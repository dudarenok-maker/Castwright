# Castwright 1.15.0

- **✨ The headlines.** Voice cloning arrives, on both speech engines — record a short sample you have permission to use and cast it like any other voice, in whatever language your book is written in. A character keeps their identity through a re-analysis, so a chapter you have already narrated can no longer quietly hand their lines to the narrator. Castwright reclaims graphics-card memory by itself before it ever tells you there is no room. And re-recording one character can no longer put their voice on somebody else's lines. Everything below, in more detail.
- **Castwright now works out what language your book is in by reading the book — not its copyright page.** Until now, the language was decided from the opening pages of the file. That sounds sensible until you remember what's actually there: the title page, the copyright notice, the dedication, the contents, and very often a foreword written by somebody else, in another language entirely. A German novel behind a short English foreword was read as an English book — and once that decision was made there was no screen anywhere to change it, so every voice, every pronunciation and every fallback for that book was wrong from then on, quietly. Castwright now looks at your book's actual chapters, skips the front matter it already knows how to recognise, works out the language of each chapter on its own, and weighs the answer by how much of the book is actually written in each language — not by how many chapters happen to agree. A book that only gives Castwright one chapter to work with — a plain file with no chapter breaks, or an ebook built as a single continuous document — still gets read whole, front matter included, because there's no chapter break to separate it from the story. A book left with just one chapter once the rest are set aside is different: the front matter was already recognised and set aside, so only that remaining chapter gets read. Either way, Castwright now needs a decent amount of text before it will trust the answer — a short book, however many chapters it has, can come back undecided instead of guessed at. Books you've already imported keep the language they have — nothing is re-decided behind your back.
- **Your cast list now keeps your book's own spelling of every name.** Castwright reads your book and works out who speaks in it — but it was never actually asking for the names back in the language the book is written in. Most of the time that was fine. Then it quietly stopped being fine: a Russian novel came back with its cast listed as "Anton Gorodovsky" and "Boris Ignatievich" instead of Антон Городецкий and Борис Игнатьевич — the same book, the same settings, just a different day. It looks like a small thing on the cast screen, but those names are also what Castwright leans on to tell your characters apart as it works through your chapters, and the first chapter's spelling gets handed to every chapter after it. Castwright now asks for names exactly as your book spells them, so what you see is what your book says.
- **A book that comes back with everybody's dialogue read by the narrator is now caught while it's being made — not after you listen to it.** Castwright works through your book a chapter at a time and checks its own work as it goes: that nothing was dropped, nothing was repeated, that the chapter reaches its real ending. What it never checked was whether your *cast* had actually been used. So when a Russian novel came back with virtually every spoken line handed to the narrator, every one of those checks passed happily — the words were all there, in the right order, nothing missing — and Castwright spent the best part of a day building what amounted to a single-voice audiobook without a murmur. For books that mark speech with a dash — Russian, French and Spanish — Castwright now notices when the spoken lines are going to the narrator instead of the people saying them, has another go at that stretch of the chapter, and tells you if it still can't get it right. It only counts lines that are genuinely somebody speaking: a dash-led aside like «— said Yegor» is narration, and always was, so it's never held against the count. Books in languages that mark speech with quotation marks are untouched.
- **Clone a voice you have permission to use — and cast it anywhere.** Record or upload a short sample, confirm it's yours (or a family member's, with their say-so), and Castwright builds a reusable voice you can hand to any character.
- **Build your own stable of narrators in My voices.** Design a voice once — from a persona, with a live audition — and it's yours to cast in any book, not just the one you designed it for. Not happy with a take? Redesign it and compare old against new side by side before you commit. Fallen for a voice you built for one character? Save it to My voices and hand it to someone else next time, in a different book entirely.
- **A cloned voice tells you clearly when it can't be used — instead of quietly rendering as someone else.** If Castwright can't rebuild a cloned voice as itself, it stops and says so, right down to the reason — a revoked voice, a missing sample, or a book set to the wrong engine — so you always know a chapter used the real voice or didn't run at all. That promise now holds on **both** of Castwright's speech engines, not just one: until now a cloned voice only worked reliably on one of them, and casting it on a book that used the other could either hit a wall or, worse, quietly get you a stand-in.
- **A cloned voice now reads a non-English book in that book's language.** Cast a voice you cloned into a German or Russian book and every line came out in English-sounding nonsense — not accented, not imperfect, but unintelligible. Your clone is language-neutral: it always could have read German, nothing was ever telling it to. Now the book decides, so the same cloned voice reads whatever you cast it into. Clones you made before this update still work and need no attention — though My voices will keep labelling them English until you re-record or rebuild them, because the language they were recorded in wasn't something Castwright kept a note of at the time. New clones are labelled with the language you actually spoke.
- **Fixing a misheard word in your voice sample now actually counts.** When you record a sample for cloning, Castwright writes down what it heard — and if it misheard you, you can correct it. Until now those corrections were quietly ignored, and the clone was built against the wrong words. Your edit is now what the voice is built from, and it sticks, so a later touch-up doesn't undo it.
- **Cloning a voice for a child now records who actually gave consent — not the child.** Cloning a family member's or a child's voice used to write the consent record as if the person being cloned had attested to it themselves, which got the guardian relationship exactly backwards. The clone wizard now asks who's attesting whenever it isn't the person themselves, and that's the name that gets recorded — and a clone on someone else's behalf can no longer be recorded without it at all. If the name is missing, Castwright refuses the clone rather than quietly filing the consent under the wrong person.
- **Revoking a cloned voice now really does erase it.** Your original recording, and everything Castwright built from it, is gone for good — not just the visible entry. Castwright tells you exactly what you're about to lose and asks you to confirm before it happens. Revoke also holds up under an unusual spelling of a voice's own key: Castwright now refuses to play a voice under anything but its canonical name, closing a corner where a copy could keep working after a revoke reported everything erased.
- **Your consent record for a cloned voice is now enforced on every path that could carry it.** That check now reaches the last couple of paths inside your own install that hadn't been covered: linking one character's voice to another's, and a plain cast save. A cast save that simply leaves a cloned character's voice out no longer silently drops it — it's restored — and one that tries to swap it for something else is refused, and refused as a plain refusal rather than a crash. A voice you've revoked, or never gave consent for in the first place, can't be quietly carried across any of them.
- **A cloned voice can no longer be silently overwritten by a redesign.** Editing a cloned voice's persona used to be able to replace it in place with a completely different, synthesised voice — while everything on screen kept calling it by the original name and nothing warned you. Redesigning or promoting a cloned voice is now refused outright, so the voice you cloned stays the one you get.
- **Designing your whole cast at once no longer knocks a cloned character off their clone.** A bulk "Design full cast" used to be able to retarget an already-cloned character onto a fresh, uncloned voice without telling you. It now skips them and names who, right in the summary — and designing a single already-cloned character on its own is refused with a reason instead of quietly doing the wrong thing.
- **Emotion variants are no longer offered for a cloned voice — because they were quietly giving you the wrong person.** Castwright can mint whisper/angry/excited/sad takes of a designed voice. Offered on a *cloned* voice, it built them from the wrong identity: either someone else's voice entirely, or nothing at all — and the take it saved was filed under a name narration never looks for, so the emotion you'd just waited for never turned up in the finished chapter. Those controls are now replaced by a line telling you why, on the character's profile and in a bulk run's summary. This is deliberately a refusal rather than a repair: an emotion variant isn't the recording you gave permission for, it's a fresh performance built in that person's voice — and one that revoking your consent wouldn't have erased. Emotion variants of a designed voice are unchanged.
- **Assigning a cloned voice to a Coqui-cast character no longer gets refused over a Qwen problem that was never going to matter.** Castwright used to check only the Qwen side of a cloned voice before letting you assign it — so a voice that was perfectly fine on Coqui could still get turned away, purely because its (unused) Qwen side wasn't ready. It now checks the engine the character is actually cast on. If the *other* engine's side of that voice isn't usable, you're told at the moment you assign it — not left to discover it later if you ever switch that character's engine.
- **A cloned voice that won't work on your chosen speech engine is now caught the moment you approve your cast — not partway through a render.** Switch your book to a different speech engine, or clone a voice from a sample with no script attached, and "Approve cast & start generating" now checks every cloned voice against the engine your book will actually use, right there before anything starts recording. If one can't render, Castwright names the character, the engine, and exactly why — then offers a real fix on the spot: add the missing transcript, try the rebuild again, cast that character on your other speech engine instead, or pick a different voice. A voice whose consent was withdrawn, or whose original sample is gone, can't be repaired, so Castwright says so plainly rather than offering a button that wouldn't work. And if you'd rather deal with it later, **Proceed anyway** still gets your book generating.
- **A chapter that needs to rebuild a voice now shows you it's happening.** Before narration starts, Castwright sometimes has to quietly re-derive a cloned voice or self-heal a designed one — which could look like nothing was happening for several seconds. The Generate screen now shows a "Preparing voice" step and names the character, so a quiet pause reads as progress, not a stall.
- **Cloned voices now derive on Coqui regardless of how your FFmpeg was built.** Rebuilding a cloned voice for the Coqui engine used a step that, on some installs — including the plain, standard way most people install FFmpeg on Windows — failed every single time, and pointed you at the wrong speech engine to fix it. Castwright now reads and rebuilds your sample itself, the same way it already does everywhere else, so that step is gone entirely. If a clone genuinely can't be rebuilt, the message now names the engine that actually failed and tells you to re-run the rebuild and check the log — and the offline Help page describing this error, which had only listed the older fixes (re-upload your sample, restore consent, switch engine, or reassign the voice), now lists that one too.
- **A cloned voice on the Coqui engine no longer fails outright on a long line — Japanese included.** Rendering a longer sentence on a voice you cloned for Coqui could come back as a plain internal error instead of audio — a dependency Castwright needed for splitting long lines into speakable chunks wasn't being installed. It's installed alongside Coqui now, so this just works — for anyone who installs (or reinstalls) Coqui from here on. Japanese needed a second, larger piece for the same job (its own dictionary for finding word boundaries in unspaced text) — that now ships in the same step too, so a cloned Japanese voice reading a long line works the same way every other language does. **If you already have Coqui set up, this one doesn't reach you automatically:** re-run the Coqui install from Model Manager, or press **Repair** on it, to pick up the fix.
- **A voice-clone build that collided with Castwright freeing up graphics-card memory** used to fail with an unhelpful error. It now either recovers on its own or tells you plainly that the model was unloaded and to try again.
- **A line attributed to a character Castwright doesn't recognise no longer renders in silence about it.** If a line in your manuscript is attributed to a name that doesn't match anyone in your cast, Castwright has always read it in the narrator's voice rather than skip it — a reasonable stand-in. What it didn't do was tell you: that substitution used to leave no trace anywhere you'd see it. It now shows up on the Cast screen, naming which id(s) it couldn't place, so you can go check the manuscript instead of finding out by ear. And if your narrator is a voice you cloned, Castwright no longer borrows it for one of these lines at all — it stops and tells you, the same way it already refuses to let any cloned voice quietly stand in for someone else.
- **And now you can actually fix it — tell Castwright the two are the same person.** The Cast screen has been naming the character ids it couldn't place, and letting you say "not the same character" when it guessed a pairing wrong. What it never let you say was **yes**. So an id you could recognise at a glance — a character whose name simply got written down two different ways between one analysis and the next — had no way back to their own lines, and kept being read by the narrator. Pick who they are from the list and press **Link to this character**, and every line they've already recorded is theirs again, with no re-analysis and no re-recording. Change your mind and it's one click back. Castwright still won't let you link one of its shared "unknown man / unknown woman" placeholders to a specific person, and says why — several different characters can be speaking under one of those at once.
- **Castwright now works out who's speaking far more reliably in books that punctuate dialogue with dashes.** In Russian — and any book that opens a line of speech with a dash rather than quote marks — Castwright has a second pass that double-checks the speaker of every line and fixes the ones it can. On these books that pass was quietly giving up: to correct a chapter it first has to match each sentence back to its exact place in your manuscript, and a single very common short reply like "— Да." could latch onto the wrong copy of itself further down the page, taking every sentence after it with it. Below a certain confidence Castwright stops correcting that chapter entirely and only marks lines for you to look at — which, on the very books this was built for, is what kept happening. Matching is now pinned in place by the long, distinctive sentences around each short one, so a short reply can't wander. On a full Russian novel this took the affected chapters from as low as 2% matched to over 92%, and every chapter now clears the bar — so the corrections actually reach your cast list instead of being withheld. Books you've already analysed will pick this up next time you re-analyse them.
- Books converted from plain text sometimes arrive with their paragraph breaks
  missing. Castwright used to read those chapters as narration and quietly
  reassign the dialogue to the narrator. Now it keeps the speaker it found and
  marks the line for your review instead — so a rough conversion costs you a
  read-through, not your cast.
- **A character's lines could quietly come out in the narrator's voice after you re-analysed the book, with nothing on screen to explain it — now fixed end to end.** Castwright privately keeps a short tag for each character, separate from the name you see, and re-analysing a book can occasionally come back with that tag spelled very slightly differently — even though the character themselves never changed. When that happened, a chapter you'd already narrated could no longer tell whose line it was reading, and quietly filled it in with the narrator instead — the chapter finished normally, and nothing said a substitution had happened. Three things change. Castwright now recognises the same character across a spelling change like that, so re-rendering the chapter puts the right voice back. Re-analysing no longer picks a fresh tag for a character it already recognises, so the problem stops being created in the first place. And the Cast screen now tells you about every tag that has drifted — what it reconciled on its own, quietly, in a section you can open if you're curious, and what it couldn't confidently match, named plainly with a picker so you can say who those lines actually belong to. If Castwright's automatic fix ever guesses wrong, "Not the same character" tells it so — and only for that one pairing, so a name it got right for someone else still gets found automatically later. Clicked the wrong one, or changed your mind? An Undo right on the same row puts it back exactly as it was, no confirmation dialog in the way — unless Castwright has since found a different, better match for that same tag on its own, in which case Undo leaves that one alone rather than replacing it with the one you'd already said no to. If the same drift showed up as more than one row on screen, Undo now clears the reminder off every row it applies to, not just the one you clicked. Saying no to a match is sturdier now too: if the character you rejected is later renamed onto the very character you compared them with, the private note behind that decision no longer outlives its own reason for existing — both are cleared together, instead of silently going on blocking a correct match between the two of them forever. If rejecting a match is ever interrupted partway through, Undo can now still clear it rather than leaving a reminder stuck with no way to remove it. And if you said "Not the same character" about the same tag for two different people, both of those decisions are now put back when Castwright repairs them — until now one of them could quietly go unenforced from then on, however many times you re-analysed the book. And a "Not the same character" reminder for someone who's since been removed from your cast no longer sits there with an Undo button that could never work — it's cleared away instead. Some of what Castwright reconciles on its own is now flagged more precisely, too: a tag it matched automatically can still be sitting on audio recorded before the match existed, so those rows now say plainly that they resolve correctly today but the existing recording may still need a re-render — instead of reading as fully settled. One thing to know: a book affected before this update doesn't heal on its own — it still needs a fresh re-render to put the right voice back.
- **The Cast screen now tells you which of those auto-reconciled rows are actually fine — and the repair checklist shrinks as you fix them, instead of listing the same rows forever.** The note above could only say a row *resolves*, with a hedge that "the existing recording may still need a re-render" — true, but not something you could act on, since Castwright itself couldn't yet tell a row that was already re-rendered apart from one that wasn't. It can now: the auto-reconciled section on the Cast screen splits into **"audio is current"** and **"audio needs a re-render"**, and the same answer now also drives the offline repair checklist, so re-rendering a flagged chapter takes its row off the list for good instead of it coming back on the very next run. Two things to know about a book you have already narrated. Its existing recordings carry no record of *when* they were made relative to the rename, so Castwright cannot yet vouch for them — they stay on the list as needing attention until you re-render them, which is the cautious direction rather than a shrug. And a line that was quietly read by the narrator because Castwright couldn't place the character at the time now stays flagged even if that character's name has since come back, because the recording really is the narrator's voice no matter how neatly the name resolves today.
- **Creating a new character can no longer steal a merged-away character's already-recorded lines.** Merge two characters into one, then later create a brand-new character under the name the older one used, and Castwright could hand that new, empty character the exact same private tag — quietly moving every line the original had already recorded off the person you merged them into. Castwright now notices the tag is still spoken for and gives the new character a different one instead, so a merge you've already made keeps protecting the audio it covers. The same protection now also covers a rarer path to the same outcome: a re-analysis can occasionally let a character quietly drop out of your cast with no record of it ever having existed, and a much later re-analysis could then hand a brand-new character that vanished character's private tag by coincidence — silently attaching all of the original's already-recorded audio to someone who never earned it. Castwright now notices that stale tag is no longer pointing at anyone and clears it on its own before it can be reused this way.
- **The one-time pass that repairs already-drifted private tags now honours a "Not the same character" decision the same precise way the Cast screen does.** It used to treat that decision as ruling out every possible match for that id, forever — even a later, genuinely correct one — so saying no to one wrong guess could quietly stop the pass from ever fixing a different, right one. It now only rules out the specific pairing you said no to. And if the character you said no to is later renamed, that decision follows them to their new name instead of quietly stopping to apply.
- **Undo, on a "Not the same character" decision, now tells you when it couldn't fully put things back.** That Undo restores the private tag Castwright had stashed away at the moment you rejected the match — but if the character that tag pointed to has since disappeared from your cast entirely, with no trace left behind, restoring it would have quietly pointed your reversal at someone who no longer exists. Castwright now catches that and says so right in the same confirmation, naming the tag it couldn't bring back, instead of restoring it as if nothing were wrong. One thing to know: your "not the same character" decision is undone either way — this only affects whether the specific tag behind it can come back with it.
- **An analysis that stops part-way no longer risks a character's recorded audio ending up in the narrator's voice.** Castwright saves its progress to your book folder while a long analysis is still running, so you can check in on it mid-way. If that in-progress save happened to notice a character's private tag had changed — the same tag drift the fix above catches — and the analysis then stopped before finishing (a connection drop, a content filter, or simply pausing it), the changed tag could stick around with nothing recording what happened, silently orphaning that character's already-recorded lines to the narrator. The in-progress save no longer makes that call at all; only a finished analysis does.
- **If something else changes your cast while an analysis is running, Castwright now tells you — instead of quietly keeping the older version.**
- **Re-recording one character no longer risks giving their lines to someone else's voice.** If you re-analysed a book after it was already narrated, asking Castwright to re-record a single character could quietly hand some of those lines to whoever the newer analysis thinks is speaking — in the voice of the character you asked for, dropped into their place in the chapter. Nothing looked wrong: the chapter finished, and every record of it named the right voice. Castwright now notices that the narration and the analysis have drifted apart, stops, and tells you how many lines changed hands — so you can re-run analysis or re-render the chapter and decide for yourself, rather than have it guessed at. Books you generate in one pass were never affected.
- **Re-recording a character now tells you when one of their voices had to be dropped.** If a voice you'd designed turns out to have been built for a different language than the book you're fixing, Castwright sets it aside rather than reading the line in the wrong language — and it now says so on screen. It was already doing the setting-aside quietly; you just never heard about it, so a line could come back in a voice you didn't choose with no explanation.
- **Fix a flagged chapter without regenerating the whole thing.** When Castwright marks a finished chapter "Suspect" — a line that came out silent, clipped, or the wrong length — the chapter row now has a button that re-records just those lines and stitches them back in. It keeps your previous take, so you can always go back. Castwright has been able to do this under the hood for a while; there was simply no way to ask for it.
- **A background repair or splice can no longer mark the wrong book as done.** Finishing one while you'd already switched books could stamp another book's same-numbered chapter as freshly rendered. And a repair that hits an unexpected error no longer leaves its spinner running forever — it now fails cleanly, the same way a normal finish does.
- **The audio-repair button is now a proper tap target on tablets**, instead of shrinking below the size your thumb needs.
- **Take a library voice back off a character.** Until now there was no way to undo an assignment — you could put a My-voices entry on a character, but never remove just that link, short of picking a different voice over it. There's a Remove-voice control right in the profile drawer now.
- **Assigning a voice from My voices now tells you what actually happened.** Trying to assign a library voice Castwright can't use on that character — the wrong engine, say — used to fail with no visible response at all. And an assignment Castwright could only write to one of your book's two engines used to close the panel with no notice either, leaving it half-done with nothing on screen to say so. Both now show the reason, and which part of the assignment stuck, right where you made it.
- **Assigning a designed voice built for a different language now warns you on the spot.** A designed voice bakes in the language you designed it in, and using it on a book in a different language doesn't come out sounding "off" — it comes out unintelligible. Until now that only came to light after a full chapter had already rendered. Castwright now tells you the moment you assign the voice, names both languages and the character, and tells you how to fix it — and the assignment still goes through, so you're never blocked from doing it on purpose.
- **Promoting a voice into your library can no longer lose the one it's replacing.** Promoting a redesigned voice removed the live version first and only afterwards checked whether the redesign meant to replace it actually existed — so promoting a second time, before a first promote had produced anything new, could delete your live voice for nothing. It now checks first.
- **Deleting a library voice now only claims success when it actually erased everything.** If a stray file couldn't be removed, Castwright used to say the voice was gone anyway. It's honest about it now, and keeps the entry around so you — and Castwright's own consent checks — can still see it and try again.
- **Voice previews now play in the engine you picked for that character, at the quality your book is set to render in** — so the cast list matches the book you're about to make, and the same voice sounds the same wherever you play it: on a cast row, on its card in My voices, while you're designing it, and on both sides of a redesign comparison. A preview also uses whichever copy of a voice is actually ready, instead of always reaching for the Qwen one and failing on a voice that genuinely had something to play, and it keeps matching the book right after you've picked a different voice over a clone. (A character you've pinned to a higher quality than the rest of the book still previews at the book's quality.)
- Auditioning a voice you just designed no longer re-records it. The take from the design was already sitting there; now it actually gets used, so the first play is instant.
- The higher-quality 1.7B voice model is greyed out until you've actually downloaded it, instead of failing partway into a run.
- Previewing a voice will now put away a voice model it isn't using to make room, rather than giving up when your graphics card looks full. If something it can't put away is in the way, it tells you which model that is and where the button to stop it lives — instead of just saying the card is full.
- **The Voices list stops telling you it's empty when it's simply filtered.** Narrow it to Russian and then look at "This book", and if that book has no Russian voices the list used to go blank and invite you to go and set up a book — on a library that was perfectly full — while quietly taking the language buttons away with it, leaving a filter still switched on and nothing on screen to switch it off. The same happened when the last voice in the language you'd picked went away while you were looking at it. Now it says plainly that nothing matches, tells you which filter is doing it, and leaves every button you'd need to undo it right there on screen. The "Variants" filter got the same treatment, and it needed it more: switching your speech engine away from Qwen used to make its buttons vanish with the filter still on, emptying the whole list with no way back short of reloading the page.
- When you link a character to the same person from an earlier book, they no longer arrive carrying a voice engine this book can't use. Linking copies the earlier character's engine across, and if that book was in another language the engine came too — invisibly. The picker showed "Default", so there was nothing to see and nothing to correct, but saving the character quietly wrote the old engine back. The picker and what gets saved now agree. Your books always rendered correctly regardless — Castwright picks the right engine at recording time no matter what the character says. (Characters picked up automatically from earlier books in a series were never affected: Castwright already refuses to carry a voice across a language boundary there.)
- **A Russian, French, or Spanish line of dialogue that opens with a dash is no longer read straight through as if the dash weren't there, on the Coqui speech engine.** That opening dash is the standard way these languages mark the start of a line of dialogue. Castwright's text prep was turning every dash into a comma before handing a line to the speech engine — including one at the very start of a line, where there's nothing before it for a comma to follow. A leading dash now becomes a leading ellipsis instead, the same way written prose already marks a pause before speech resumes.
- **The Coqui speech engine now catches the rare line that comes back implausibly short, and quietly tries again with a fresh take.** Under unlucky conditions a short line could occasionally render as barely any audio at all. Castwright's other speech engine already caught this; Coqui now does too, with no visible interruption when the retry succeeds. On the rare occasion a line is still stuck after that retry, Castwright now stops and reports it rather than shipping the bad audio — the usual chapter-level retry clears it the great majority of the time.
- **A rarer Coqui glitch — a line that comes back full-length and fluent, but in the wrong language entirely — is now caught too, when line-by-line accuracy checking is turned on.** The too-short catch above can't see this one: the audio sounds perfectly normal, just says the wrong thing. Castwright's accuracy check already listens to every line and compares it against your manuscript, but used to give a wrong-language line the benefit of the doubt rather than risk flagging a perfectly good line that just happened to be awkward to make out. It now recognises the difference — a line that's both hard to make out *and* bears no resemblance at all to what your book actually says gets re-recorded, the same as any other clearly wrong line would.
- **Cloning a voice and then rendering it on the other speech engine could fail with a confusing error — fixed.** Clone a voice (which checks it against your sample first), then render a chapter on the other engine, and that render could fail outright until you restarted Castwright. It's fixed at the source now, so the sequence just works. The status check that used to say the engine was ready even when this could happen has also been made more honest, and the error message you'd have seen — which wrongly blamed a missing piece of software — no longer misleads. One trade-off worth knowing: if you have Coqui installed, Castwright's speech service now takes about twelve seconds longer to start up, because it does some of its loading up front to head this off. If you don't use Coqui, nothing changes — the extra step is skipped entirely.
- **Castwright now tells a voice engine that isn't installed apart from one that's installed but won't start — and says the same thing everywhere.** A speech engine can be sitting right there on disk and still be unable to run, and until now Castwright only noticed that for one of its engines: for Kokoro, Qwen and the listening-check model it just saw the files, called them installed, and said nothing — you'd find out when a chapter failed. Castwright now remembers what actually happened the last time it tried to start each one, so a broken install shows up as broken and the **Repair** button is there to press. The three places that report this now agree with each other: the Health board in Admin, the checklist you see the first time you set up Castwright — which used to call both faults the same thing and tell you to "repair" either way, the wrong instruction for something that was never there — and Model Manager's own buttons, which now offer **Install** for a package that was never installed and **Repair** only for one that is there and won't load. One honest limit: Castwright learns this by trying, so an engine you've never used yet still shows only what's on disk.
- **"Not enough GPU memory" no longer shows up when the memory is actually free.** After finishing a chapter, Castwright could leave a few gigabytes of graphics memory set aside but unused — and then refuse the next thing you asked for, whether that was designing a voice or checking a line, because from the outside that memory looked occupied. Restarting the voice engine was the only way to clear it. It now finishes writing everything else it was in the middle of — so the book is never left half-updated — and then stops and tells you the run didn't complete, so you can retry rather than find out later. The same treatment now reaches every other place that wait could land. **An analysis that couldn't save your cast no longer reports as finished.** If the book was busy at the exact moment Castwright went to write the final cast list, the run used to end with a tick while that list and your chapter details were never saved at all — so the next thing you opened showed the previous run's work and nothing said why. It now tells you the run didn't complete, so you re-run it there and then. The progress snapshots Castwright takes while a long analysis is still going are the deliberate exception: the final save overwrites them anyway, so one of those being blocked costs you nothing and no longer costs you the run. **A busy book now reads as busy, not broken.** Designing your whole cast, generating personas for everyone, applying a change to a character across a series, assigning a voice across linked books, and the script review all report results one item at a time — and when one item was blocked by something else holding that book, the line you were shown described it as a failure of that chapter or that character. It now says plainly that something else had the book and that this one is worth another go. **Anything that fails because the book was busy now says so, in words you can act on** instead of a blank "something went wrong" — a merge, an analysis, assigning a voice, removing one from **My voices**, and every one of the item-by-item jobs above. And wherever that message appears it is the same plain sentence: **nothing about where your files live, or what Castwright calls its own internal machinery, ever ends up on screen** — that detail goes to the log, where it is actually useful. **Accepting a suggested merge that never ran no longer loses the suggestion.** If the book was busy at the moment you pressed Accept, the merge didn't happen — but the suggestion was removed anyway, so there was nothing left to press and only a re-analysis brought it back. Castwright now tells the two apart: the suggestion stays put unless the merge really did land, so you just try again. And if you tell Castwright "not the same character" and the tidy-up afterwards gets blocked, it now says so: your decision is saved and safe, and pressing the button again finishes the rest — which matters here because, unlike the other half of that pair, you can't count on anything later doing it for you.
- **An export that runs into trouble can no longer take Castwright down with it.** While Castwright was packing a download — a zip of your chapters — one of the audio files it was reading could stop being readable underneath it: its permissions changed, or something cleaned the folder up mid-build. Instead of that one download failing and telling you so, Castwright stopped dead, and anything else running at the time went with it — including a book part-way through narration. Now the export fails on its own and says what went wrong, and everything else carries on.
- **Dialogue written with single quotes is no longer cut off mid-sentence.** If your book puts speech in single quotes — as most British publishers do — Castwright was ending every line of dialogue at the first apostrophe, so ‘I don’t know,’ she said became just *I don*, and the rest of the line was read as narration in the narrator's voice. Contractions, names like O’Brien, and dialect spellings such as ’em and ’cause are now understood as part of the speech rather than the end of it.

- **A passage Castwright can't make sense of now gets broken into smaller pieces instead of being re-read word-for-word until the allowance runs out.** Castwright checks its own work on each chapter and re-reads a passage when the check fails — which usually fixes it, because the problem is normally a one-off. But when a passage defeated it the *same* way every single time, re-reading was pointless: it would spend its whole allowance getting the identical result, then hand the chapter back with a bad reading and a note suggesting you try again — advice that could never work, since trying again did exactly the same thing. Castwright now notices it has hit the exact same wall twice, stops there, and re-reads the passage as two shorter ones instead. That is a genuinely different task, so it usually succeeds where repetition could not — and where it can't divide the passage any further, it stops rather than spending the rest of its allowance proving the point.

- **A dash-opened line of Spanish or French dialogue could read as narration, depending on which invisible character its EPUB happened to carry.** Some ebook conversions leave a dash written out as its HTML code rather than the dash itself — Castwright already understood one such code in these two languages but not its sibling. It now understands both, so the same dash reads as dialogue either way.
- **Groundwork for recognising more of the world's quotation styles.** Some languages mark speech more than one way — a main style, and a secondary one that shows up now and then. Castwright's dialogue reader can now tell the two apart without letting them get tangled together, so a future style can be added safely, without any risk to the dialogue it already reads correctly today. Nothing about how your book sounds changes yet — this is the groundwork that makes the next step possible.

- **A book whose EPUB spelled out its punctuation in HTML code — a dash, an accented letter, a curly quote — no longer had any of it read aloud letter by letter.** Some ebook conversions write "—" as `&mdash;`, "é" as `&eacute;`, and so on, instead of the character itself. Castwright already understood a handful of these codes; it now understands the complete set, so a chapter title or a line of dialogue that used one comes out exactly as written, dash, accent, or quote mark included — and a book that was imported before this fix picks up the correction the next time Castwright re-reads the file — straight away if you **re-parse** it from its menu in your library, or on a later analysis that reloads it from disk.

- **Behind the scenes: Castwright can now measure how much of a book's dialogue is quietly being read by the narrator instead of its own characters.** This doesn't change anything you see or hear yet — no badge, no warning, nothing new on screen — but it's the groundwork for a future one that will actually tell you when a book has slipped into this state, so it stops sitting unnoticed until you happen to hear it for yourself.
